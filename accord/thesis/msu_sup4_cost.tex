\label{sup:s4}

\subsection{What the Controls Cost}

Table~\ref{sup:tab:disc-cost} itemises it.
\label{sup:sec:disc-cost}

Nobody reports this, so it is reported here, with the limit stated first.
\textbf{These figures are compute only.} Person-hours were not logged for most
of the project and are not reconstructed, because a reconstructed estimate
would be an invented number in a thesis about invented numbers.

The control that retracted the directional core of three corrections cost
$14.2$ GPU-minutes, and the convergence sweep that licenses the whole
argument of Chapter 5 of the thesis cost $555$ minutes on its own; the
reconciled total across every control is $1216.4$ GPU-minutes and $79$
runs. Controls are not always this cheap: re-annotation
is not, a second annotator is not, and one control here cannot be
recomputed from disk and requires re-running the experiment that produced
it. The claim is narrower: in this project the controls that reversed
conclusions cost less compute than the experiments whose conclusions they
reversed, and cost is not why they went unrun.

No comparison against prose checklists is offered, and no claim is made that
executable gates outperform them. The nearest thing to evidence on that
question comes from journal peer review rather than from here, where
introducing an automated statistical consistency check is associated with a
steep decline in reporting inconsistencies \citep{nuijten2023statcheck},
measured over a corpus this project cannot approach.
