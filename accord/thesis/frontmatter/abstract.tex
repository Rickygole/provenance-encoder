  A nursing note and a discharge summary can disagree about a medication, and only the summary leaves
the hospital. This thesis audits the pipeline the field would build for detecting them in intensive
care records: rule-based mining, language-model weak supervision, a purpose-built detector, and
expert evaluation at a 4 percent confirmed base rate. Every stage returns a negative or bounded
verdict. Candidate precision is not established, because the selector chose the displayed evidence by
position; four readings put the resulting defect rate between 0 and 0.9315, two of them near 0.4 as is
the rebuilt pool's design rate, with a lower bound of 72 of 165 annotated pairs. Credited architectural components are unsupported by
code audit. A detector ensemble retrained on subject-disjoint rows is indistinguishable from chance
on 253 non-ambiguous pairs, which resolve only effects above 0.7466 in area under the curve, and one
annotator leaves that null fragile: 1 or 2 targeted relabels undo it. A marginal feasibility gate
offered here as a pre-training check was validated and failed as a screen, its sensitivity to student
failure reaching 0.2941 against a registered floor of 0.90 and reducing at this pilot size to a
threshold on a flag count. Graded labeller scores rank above chance where their hard labels cannot,
but none separates from a score carrying only the generating rule's identity. Five of the twelve
confirmed contradictions are an anticoagulant, as are all three rated most dangerous. Each failure
here names the measurement that would have caught it.
