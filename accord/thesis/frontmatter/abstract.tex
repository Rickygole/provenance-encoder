  A nursing note and a discharge summary can disagree about a medication,
and only the summary leaves the hospital.
This thesis audits a four-stage pipeline detecting such
contradictions in an intensive care database.
First, three of the four stage readings failed their checks (one
not established, two unsupported); the fourth, the evaluation's near-chance
reading, survived as a bounded null; one annotator leaves it fragile. Second, candidate precision is not established;
the labeller's agreement was indistinguishable from chance; credited architectural components are an
initialisation artifact, a metric with its target in its input and an
untrained module; a detector ensemble retrained on subject-disjoint rows is
indistinguishable from chance on 253 non-ambiguous pairs, also on
reproducible labels, resolving only area
under the curve effects above 0.75 to 0.81. Third, a check runnable before
training was applied after the fact: a labeller flagging 261 of 299 pairs,
against 12 a pharmacist confirmed, could not have rejected chance, its smallest
attainable one-sided significance being 0.1894; the check can rule a labeller
out, never in. Fourth, trigger-exemplar dissociation is a mining bug pattern: on the rebuilt candidate pool, the original positional selector shows an exemplar other than the mention that fired for 41.4 percent of Rules 1 and 2 candidates, a design rate, not the annotated exemplars' unrecoverable defect rate; an assertion catches it once the schema records which mention fired. Pipeline builders
should measure the labeller's positive rate and agreement on a small
expert-labelled sample before training. Unsolvability is not shown.
