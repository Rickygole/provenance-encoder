  A contradiction between a nursing note and a discharge summary can carry a medication instruction out of the hospital that the care team had already stopped. This thesis builds a standard three stage pipeline for detecting such contradictions in an intensive care database: rule-based candidate mining, a large language model used as a weak annotator, and a purpose-built neural detector. The pipeline does not work. This thesis runs the control at each stage that separates real failure from measurement artifact. The weak annotator's marginal runs far ahead of the confirmed rate, fifteen to twenty-four times too often at eight billion parameters and four to five at frontier scale, so the failure is general and its size belongs to the labeller. Three architectural components credited with the gains are falsified by inspecting the code behind their numbers, not by a failed ablation. A survey measures how often published work audits the selection step upstream of the model it evaluates, and finds it rarely done; it says nothing about the other checks this thesis applied. A positive control, adjudicated by a rule committed before the sweep ran, shows the approach resolves a real difference on a public benchmark. The detector ranks no better than chance on the annotated pairs, a null its own class counts leave informative only above a stated minimum effect. The work does not show the task is unsolvable. It contributes three transferable checks, a method for turning a failed pipeline into a diagnosed one, and their cost.\par}
