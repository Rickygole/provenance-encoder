  A contradiction between a nursing note and a discharge summary can carry a medication instruction out of the hospital that the care team had already stopped. This thesis builds a standard three stage pipeline for detecting such contradictions in an intensive care database: rule-based candidate mining, a large language model used as a weak annotator, and a purpose-built neural detector. The pipeline does not work. This thesis runs the control at each stage that separates a real failure from an artifact of measurement. Four findings survive scrutiny. The weak annotator's marginal runs far ahead of the confirmed rate, calling contradictions fifteen to twenty-four times too often across three models. Three architectural components credited with the gains are falsified by inspecting the code behind their numbers, not by an ablation that fails to help. A survey finds the checks that would have caught these failures are rarely run. A positive control, adjudicated by a rule committed before the data existed, shows the approach resolves a real difference on a public benchmark, and under that same harness the detector itself ranks no better than chance on the annotated pairs, its null informative only above a stated minimum effect and silent below it. The work does not show the task is unsolvable. It contributes a method for turning a failed pipeline into a diagnosed one, the checks, and their cost.\par}
